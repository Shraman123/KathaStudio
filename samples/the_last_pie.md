# The Last Pie

Every November, the town of Maple Hollow, Vermont, held its Harvest Fair on the village green, and every November for thirty-one years, Martha Quinn's apple pie won the blue ribbon.

This year, Martha was not sure she would bake at all. Her husband, Walt, had died in March. It was Walt who peeled the apples in one long, unbroken curl, and Walt who hummed off-key while the crust browned. The kitchen had been very quiet since.

Her grandson Sam, who was twelve and had come to stay for Thanksgiving week, found the recipe card on the Monday. It was tucked inside a cookbook, stained with butter, in Walt's cramped handwriting.

"Grandma," he said, holding it up. "Grandpa wrote this? I thought it was your recipe."

Martha took off her glasses and wiped them slowly. "It was ours," she said. "He was the one who remembered the measurements. I just did the tasting."

"Then we have to enter," Sam said. "For him."

Martha laughed, but it came out thin. "Sweetheart, Harold Beck has been waiting three decades to beat me. He's already telling everyone at the diner that this is his year."

Harold Beck ran the hardware store on Main Street and baked a pecan pie so glossy that people photographed it. He had come second thirty-one times. He was not a bad man, only a very determined one.

"So let him try," Sam said.

---

They baked on the Thursday before the fair. Sam peeled the apples, badly at first, the peel breaking into short, sad pieces. Martha showed him how to turn the fruit instead of the knife. By the sixth apple, he had a curl that reached the floor.

"Your grandfather would have made a fuss about that," she said, and for the first time in months, she smiled without deciding to.

But something was wrong with the card. Where the spices should have been, the writing had faded to a brown smudge. Martha could read "cinnamon" and "nutmeg," but there was a third word, and it was gone.

They tried cloves. It tasted like a dentist's office. They tried ginger. It was fine, but it was not Walt's pie. By Friday evening, there were four pies on the counter, and none of them was right.

Martha sat down heavily at the kitchen table. "Maybe that's the point," she said quietly. "Maybe some things only work when both people are here."

Sam didn't answer. He went out to the porch, where Walt's old coat still hung on a hook, and put his hands in the pockets because he didn't know what else to do. In the left pocket, there was a folded receipt from Beck's Hardware, and on the back, in the same cramped handwriting: *Don't forget — a pinch of black pepper. M. never guesses.*

Sam ran inside so fast he nearly knocked over the pies.

---

The Harvest Fair was loud with brass music and the smell of cider. Harold Beck stood behind his pecan pie in a pressed shirt and a bow tie. When Martha arrived with her basket, he nodded to her stiffly.

"Martha. I was sorry about Walt."

"Thank you, Harold."

"I'll be honest," he said, lowering his voice. "I almost didn't enter. It didn't feel right, winning against you this year."

Martha looked at him for a long moment. "Walt would have hated that," she said. "He loved watching you lose."

Harold blinked, and then, to everyone's surprise, he laughed.

The judges tasted in silence. The mayor, Linda Park, took a second bite of the apple pie and closed her eyes. "There's something in this," she said. "Something warm. I can't place it."

Sam grinned at his grandmother. Martha said nothing at all.

The blue ribbon went to Martha Quinn, for the thirty-second time. The red ribbon went to Harold Beck, who shook her hand and held it a little longer than necessary.

"Next year," he said.

"Next year," she agreed.

---

That night, Martha and Sam ate the last slice at the kitchen table, sharing one fork. The house was still quiet, but it was a different kind of quiet now, the kind that is waiting for something rather than missing something.

"Grandma," Sam said, "will you teach me the whole recipe? Properly? So I remember it."

Martha reached across and pushed the hair out of his eyes.

"I'll teach you the measurements," she said. "You'll have to learn the tasting on your own."
